## Introduction

Routine women's health checkups aren't just about one test — they cover several areas of gynaecological and reproductive health, and some of the guidance around them has genuinely changed recently. This guide gives an updated overview of what a comprehensive checkup can include and when specific symptoms should prompt a visit sooner than your next scheduled appointment.

**Important:** not every test discussed here applies to every woman, every year — screening intervals and relevance depend on your age, history and individual risk factors, which your gynaecologist will help determine.

## Key Takeaways

- Cervical cancer screening guidance was updated in 2026, including a new self-collected HPV sampling option for ages 30–65.
- Routine gynaecological care covers menstrual health, contraception, fertility concerns, breast health awareness, and menopause — not just one test.
- Not every woman needs every test annually; your doctor tailors screening to your age and risk profile.
- Some symptoms warrant a visit regardless of when your next routine checkup is due.

## Routine Gynaecological Consultation

A general checkup typically includes a conversation about your menstrual history, any symptoms, contraception or family planning needs, and relevant lifestyle factors, alongside a clinical examination as appropriate.

## Menstrual Health

Reviewing your cycle regularity and any changes is a standard part of a women's health checkup. If you've noticed irregularity, our [irregular periods guide](/blog/irregular-periods-causes) covers common causes in more depth.

## Cervical Cancer Screening — What's Changed in 2026

Cervical cancer screening guidance was meaningfully updated in 2026. Under current guidance:

- Women aged **21–29** are generally recommended cervical cytology (a Pap test) every 3 years.
- Women aged **30–65** are generally offered primary HPV testing — every 5 years if the sample is collected by a clinician, using approved testing methods.
- A newer option now allows **self-collected vaginal samples** for HPV testing for women in the 30–65 age range, generally with a 3-year interval when this method is used.

This is a genuine, recent shift from older guidance, and it means screening may look a little different than what you experienced at your last checkup. Your gynaecologist can advise which approach and interval is appropriate for you.

## HPV-Related Prevention

HPV (human papillomavirus) is the main cause of cervical cancer, which is part of why HPV testing has become central to modern cervical screening. Vaccination against HPV, where relevant to your age and history, is a conversation worth having with your doctor.

## Contraception and Family Planning

Routine checkups are also a natural time to discuss contraception — whether you're looking to start, switch, or stop a method, or to talk through family planning more broadly.

## Fertility Concerns

If conceiving is on your mind, even if you're not actively trying yet, it's reasonable to raise this during a routine visit. Our [fertility evaluation guide](/blog/fertility-evaluation) explains when a more formal evaluation is typically recommended.

## Breast Health Awareness

General breast health awareness — knowing what's normal for you and noticing changes — is worth discussing with your doctor, who can advise on what monitoring is appropriate based on your age and risk factors.

## Menopause and Perimenopause

As you approach or go through menopause, checkups shift focus toward symptoms like cycle changes, hot flashes, and general wellbeing during this transition. This is a normal life stage, and support is available if symptoms are affecting your quality of life.

## Pelvic Symptoms Worth Mentioning

Symptoms such as pelvic pain, unusual discharge, pain during intercourse, or pressure sensations are all worth discussing with your gynaecologist, even if they seem minor or intermittent.

## When Symptoms Require Consultation (Don't Wait for a Routine Visit)

See a gynaecologist promptly, rather than waiting for your next scheduled checkup, if you notice:

- Abnormal vaginal bleeding (between periods, after intercourse, or after menopause)
- Persistent or severe pelvic pain
- Unusual vaginal discharge or odour
- New breast lumps or changes
- Menopause symptoms that are significantly affecting your daily life

## A Balanced Approach

Preventive care works best when it's tailored, not maximalist — you don't need every possible test every year. The goal of a women's health checkup is to keep the screenings that matter for your age and risk profile on track, while making sure new or concerning symptoms don't wait unnecessarily for a routine appointment.
